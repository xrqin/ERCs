## Abstract

In an off-chain channel, the parties lock funds in a contract once and then exchange signed state updates directly, using the chain only to open, close, or dispute the channel.
A conditional settlement is an update in which an amount is owed only if a stated condition is met before an expiry, for example a hash preimage is revealed or a verifier contract accepts a proof, and is returned to the payer otherwise.
This ERC defines [EIP-712](./eip-712.md) typed data for such a conditional obligation, for the evidence that its condition was met, and for a request that a third party submit the claim.
It also defines an on-chain interface through which the contract adjudicating a channel settles or refunds the obligation and reports its status, with optional interfaces for capability discovery and relayed claims.
Opening, funding, disputing, and closing the channel remain the responsibility of the channel framework that this interface extends.

## Motivation

Recent agent-related ERCs mainly focus on identity, accounts, permissions, or payment signaling.
They do not define a common settlement layer for high-frequency machine-to-machine interactions.

This gap matters because autonomous agents have a very different interaction profile than human users:

- They are online continuously.
- They can sign and verify state updates automatically.
- They perform recurring micropayments, streaming payments, and conditional execution.
- They benefit from sub-second, near-zero-marginal-cost settlement.

Historically, state channels have been difficult to adopt for human users because humans do not want to manage liveness, disputes, and frequent signatures.
Agents, however, are natural channel participants.

This ERC aims to standardize the settlement-facing interface of such systems without forcing a single implementation architecture.
It is designed so that different wallets, hubs, relays, private execution backends, and adjudicators can interoperate around a shared settlement envelope.

### Scope

This proposal intentionally standardizes the **settlement interface**, not the full payment channel stack.
It is designed to compose with existing off-chain channel frameworks rather than replace them.
The title, the interface names, and the EIP-712 domain name keep the word "Agent" because autonomous agents are the motivating user class, but the specification itself is actor-agnostic: nothing in it depends on who or what controls a signing key, so human signers and other automated systems can use the same interfaces.

The following are in scope:

- Proof-bound conditional obligation message formats.
- Proof reference formats.
- Relay claim request formats.
- Minimal conditional settlement extension interfaces.

The following are out of scope:

- Channel lifecycle primitives such as challenge, checkpoint, conclude, close, or dispute.
- Custody and exit primitives such as deposit, transfer, reclaim, or exit format.
- Specific payment-topology choices such as HTLCs or virtual channels.
- Routing path formats and routing algorithms.
- Specific rebalancing algorithms or coordination policies.
- Application-layer job or commerce protocols.
- Proof systems and their internal formats, including specific zero-knowledge circuits.
- Specific private execution backends or privacy-preserving routing constructions.
- Solvency proofs, margin vaults, or operator collateral models.
- The economic policy of hubs, including liquidity management strategies.

This boundary keeps the ERC narrow enough to be implementable while still being useful.

### Relationship to Existing Standards

This ERC occupies one layer of the emerging agent infrastructure stack:

- State channel frameworks that provide lifecycle and custody sit below it. This ERC is added to such a host framework as a conditional settlement extension and does not redefine lifecycle or custody primitives.
- Agent identity and reputation standards are orthogonal. They address who an agent is and whether it is trusted, while this ERC addresses how a conditional obligation is settled.
- Application protocols such as [ERC-8183](./eip-8183.md) sit above it. They define business objects such as jobs, submissions, and evaluations, and can use this ERC to resolve the conditional payments those objects give rise to.
- Signed-voucher unidirectional micropayment channels fix one payment topology. This ERC is independent of topology and of proof system, and standardizes the settlement interface rather than a channel construction.

## Specification

The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT", "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in RFC 2119 and RFC 8174.

### Roles

- `Participant`: a signer taking part in off-chain settlement, such as an autonomous agent.
- `Hub`: a coordinating counterparty or routing service that may aggregate or forward settlement.
- `Relay`: an optional third party that submits a claim or settlement transaction on behalf of a participant.
- `Host Framework`: an existing off-chain channel or settlement framework that defines lifecycle and custody primitives.
- `Adjudicator`: an on-chain contract that exposes conditional settlement extension hooks and may be composed with a host-framework adjudicator.
- `Verifier`: an on-chain contract that implements verification logic for a given proof type.

### Host Framework Composition

This ERC does not standardize `challenge`, `checkpoint`, `conclude`, `close`, `dispute`, `deposit`, `transfer`, `reclaim`, or exit format.
Where a host framework defines those methods, they remain authoritative for lifecycle and custody.

If a host framework already defines canonical lifecycle primitives, such as `challenge`, `checkpoint`, `conclude`, `close`, or `dispute`, implementations SHOULD reuse those primitives rather than expose conflicting wrappers as part of ERC compliance.

If a host framework already defines canonical custody primitives, such as deposit, transfer, reclaim, or exit handling, implementations SHOULD reuse those primitives as well.

A contract MAY offer convenience wrappers around the host framework, but such wrappers are not part of ERC compliance.

If an implementation also uses local operational states such as routing budgets or capacity budgets, those states MUST NOT override the canonical settlement state used by the host framework lifecycle.
Conflicts between operational state and canonical settlement state MUST resolve in favor of the latest valid canonical settlement state as recognized by the host-framework adjudicator.

### EIP-712 Domain

All off-chain messages standardized by this ERC MUST be signed over an [EIP-712](./eip-712.md) domain.

The domain MUST include at least:

- `name`
- `version`
- `chainId`
- `verifyingContract`

Implementations SHOULD use:

- `name = "AgentOffchainConditionalSettlement"`
- `version = "1"`

If a signer is a contract account, signatures MUST be verified via [ERC-1271](./eip-1271.md).

Implementations that support smart account signers SHOULD be aware of [ERC-6492](./eip-6492.md) (counterfactual signature verification) for signers whose contract is not yet deployed.

Implementations MUST expose [EIP-712](./eip-712.md) domain information via [ERC-5267](./eip-5267.md) (`eip712Domain()`) rather than a custom `domainSeparator()` function.

### Canonical Identifiers

#### Channel Identifier

This ERC does not require a single channel construction model, but interoperable implementations SHOULD derive `channelId` from stable settlement context rather than local database identifiers.

A RECOMMENDED derivation is:

```solidity
channelId = keccak256(abi.encode(
    block.chainid,
    address(adjudicator),
    participantSetHash,
    channelSalt
));
```

Where:

- `participantSetHash` commits to the authorized settlement participants.
- `channelSalt` distinguishes multiple settlement relationships between the same participants.

If an implementation is layered over an existing host framework that already defines a canonical `channelId`, it SHOULD reuse that identifier verbatim.

#### Request Identifier

For relay requests and capacity certificates, implementations SHOULD derive request identifiers from the full typed-data payload rather than sequential local counters alone.

### Core Typed Data

#### Condition Type Identifiers

Condition types are identified by `bytes32 conditionType`.
They describe **what proposition must be true** for the lock to be settleable, not how that proposition is proven. The verification pathway is determined by `proofType` (see below).

Implementations MAY support additional condition types, but the following identifiers are RECOMMENDED:

- `keccak256("HTLC")` — a correct hash preimage has been revealed.
- `keccak256("TIMELOCK")` — a specified timestamp has been reached.
- `keccak256("THRESHOLD_APPROVAL")` — N-of-M authorized parties have signed approval. The signer set and threshold MUST be committed in `conditionCommitment`.
- `keccak256("EXTERNAL_ASSERTION")` — an external entity (oracle, TEE enclave, off-chain program, or other asserter) has attested that a fact is true. The identity of the asserter and the asserted fact MUST be committed in `conditionCommitment`. This is a general-purpose condition: the *method* of attestation (oracle signature, ZK proof, TEE remote attestation) is specified by `proofType`, not by this identifier.
- `keccak256("COMPOSITE")` — a boolean combination of sub-conditions.

#### Proof Type Identifiers

Proof types are identified by `bytes32 proofType` in `SettlementProofRef` and `ClaimRelayRequest`.
They indicate the verification pathway that MUST be used to validate the settlement evidence, not the internal format or length of the proof itself.

Implementations MAY support additional proof types, but the following identifiers are RECOMMENDED:

- `keccak256("RECEIPT_ROOT")` — settlement is proven by a Merkle inclusion proof against a receipt or state root.
- `keccak256("ZK_PROOF")` — settlement is proven by a zero-knowledge proof verified by the `verifier` contract. The proof system (Groth16, PLONK, STARK, etc.) is determined by the verifier, not by this identifier.
- `keccak256("ORACLE_ATTESTATION")` — settlement is proven by a signed attestation from an oracle identified by the `verifier` address.
- `keccak256("MULTISIG_ATTESTATION")` — settlement is proven by a threshold or multi-signature attestation from a known signer set.
- `keccak256("TEE_ATTESTATION")` — settlement is proven by a remote attestation from a trusted execution environment.

Note: relay claim submission is an authorization and transport mechanism, not a proof family. It is defined in Optional Extension B (Private Claim Relay) and is orthogonal to proof types.

All canonical identifiers are derived via `keccak256` over a human-readable label.
Third-party extensions SHOULD follow the same convention, using a namespaced label such as `keccak256("VENDOR.CUSTOM_TYPE")` to avoid collisions.

Implementations MUST NOT interpret `proofType` as constraining the length, encoding, or circuit-level details of the proof bytes themselves. Those details are determined by the `verifier` contract referenced in `SettlementProofRef`.

#### `ConditionalLock`

`ConditionalLock` is the canonical off-chain settlement envelope.
The name is a convenience label; it should be understood as a proof-bound conditional obligation envelope, not as a requirement that every host framework expose a standalone native lock primitive.

```solidity
struct ConditionalLock {
    bytes32 channelId;
    address initiator;
    address responder;
    bytes32 assetId;
    uint256 amount;
    uint256 fee;
    uint256 expiry;
    bytes32 conditionType;
    bytes32 conditionCommitment;
    bytes32 applicationCommitment;
    bytes32 escrowCommitment;
    uint256 channelNonce;
}
```

Field requirements:

- `channelId` MUST uniquely identify the off-chain settlement relationship.
- `assetId` MUST map deterministically to the asset identity used by the host framework's custody layer. If the host framework uses token contract addresses, the RECOMMENDED derivation is `assetId = bytes32(uint256(uint160(tokenAddress)))`. If a host framework defines its own asset key (e.g., an allocation key), implementations MUST document the canonical mapping between `assetId` and the host key. There MUST NOT be two independent sources of truth for asset identity.
- `amount` MUST represent the principal to be settled.
- `fee` MUST represent the fee owed to the settlement counterparty, if any.
- `expiry` MUST be a UNIX timestamp (seconds since epoch) defining the time after which refund becomes valid if settlement has not completed. Block-number-based expiry is NOT RECOMMENDED because it is not portable across chains with different block times.
- `conditionCommitment` MUST commit to the condition-specific witness or attestation requirements.
- `applicationCommitment` MAY commit to application-specific execution details such as call parameters, constraints, or intent hashes.
- `escrowCommitment` MAY commit to a claim secret, escrow key, or claim destination abstraction.
- `channelNonce` MUST prevent stale-state replay within the channel and MAY correspond to the host framework's turn number, version counter, or equivalent monotonic lifecycle counter.

The `lockId` for a `ConditionalLock` MUST be derived as:

```solidity
lockId = keccak256(abi.encode(
    channelId,
    initiator,
    responder,
    assetId,
    amount,
    fee,
    expiry,
    conditionType,
    conditionCommitment,
    applicationCommitment,
    escrowCommitment,
    channelNonce
));
```

#### `SettlementProofRef`

`SettlementProofRef` references whatever evidence is used to settle the lock.

```solidity
struct SettlementProofRef {
    bytes32 channelId;
    bytes32 lockId;
    bytes32 proofType;
    bytes32 settlementRoot;
    bytes32 proofDigest;
    address verifier;
    bytes32 auxDataHash;
}
```

Field requirements:

- `channelId` MUST match the channel or settlement context of the referenced lock.
- `lockId` MUST reference a previously agreed `ConditionalLock`.
- `proofType` MUST identify the proof or attestation family.
- `settlementRoot` MAY represent a receipt root, attestation root, Merkle root, or other settlement anchor.
- `proofDigest` MUST commit to the opaque proof bytes or attestation payload.
- `verifier` MUST be an on-chain contract address that implements the verification logic for the given `proofType`. Off-chain verification endpoints are out of scope for this field; if off-chain verification is needed, it SHOULD be coordinated through the capability discovery layer.
- `auxDataHash` MAY commit to additional public inputs or settlement metadata.

This ERC does not standardize the contents of the proof itself.

#### `ClaimRelayRequest`

`ClaimRelayRequest` is used when a relay submits a claim on behalf of a participant.

```solidity
struct ClaimRelayRequest {
    bytes32 channelId;
    bytes32 lockId;
    bytes32 escrowCommitment;
    bytes32 outputCommitmentHash;
    uint256 maxRelayFee;
    uint256 deadline;
    bytes32 proofType;
    bytes32 proofDigest;
    address verifier;
    uint256 nonce;
}
```

Field requirements:

- `channelId` MUST match the channel or settlement context of the related conditional lock.
- `lockId` MUST reference the related conditional lock.
- `escrowCommitment` MUST match the commitment expected by the settlement flow.
- `outputCommitmentHash` MUST commit to the desired output state or minted notes.
- `maxRelayFee` MUST cap the maximum relay fee the requester is willing to pay. The fee is denominated in the same asset as the referenced `ConditionalLock.assetId`. A value of `0` indicates the meta-transaction forwarding model (see Relay Gas Model).
- `deadline` MUST be a UNIX timestamp (seconds since epoch) after which the request becomes invalid.
- `verifier` MUST match the `verifier` of the corresponding `SettlementProofRef`, binding the relay request to a specific verification contract.
- `nonce` MUST prevent relay replay.

### Signing And Activation Rules

#### Conditional Lock Activation

A `ConditionalLock` MUST NOT be considered active solely because one party signed it.

Instead, an implementation MUST define an activation rule and apply it consistently.
For bilateral channel-style systems, the RECOMMENDED rule is:

- the initiator signs the lock proposal, and
- the counterparty or hub co-signs the same lock payload before the lock becomes active.

If an implementation supports multi-party channels or delegated signers, it MUST define:

- which signers are required,
- how their signatures are ordered or aggregated,
- whether the signer set is fixed or capability-dependent.

#### Pending Lock Limits

Implementations SHOULD define:

- maximum pending locks per channel,
- minimum lock values,
- relay fee policy,
- expiry windows,
- reputation or rate limits for abusive participants.

#### Replay Protection

Implementations MUST protect against:

- replay across channels,
- replay across chains,
- replay across relays,
- replay of expired locks,
- replay of old lifecycle states.

#### Proof Reference Binding

An implementation MUST ensure that a `SettlementProofRef` is bound to exactly one `lockId`.
It MUST NOT be possible to reuse one proof reference to settle multiple incompatible locks.

#### Verifier Selection

Implementations MUST define:

- who chooses the verifier,
- how the verifier is upgraded,
- how the settlement root or proof anchor is authenticated.

#### Adapter Safety

If a system uses adapters or execution routers behind this settlement interface, implementations SHOULD use strict allowlists, typed parameter validation, exact approvals, allowance reset, reentrancy protection, and output constraint checks.

#### Relay Request Authorization

An implementation that supports `ClaimRelayRequest` MUST verify that the relay request was explicitly authorized by the claimant or the claimant's authorized contract account.

This authorization MAY be:

- an [EIP-712](./eip-712.md) signature over `ClaimRelayRequest`,
- an [ERC-1271](./eip-1271.md) contract signature check,
- an implementation-specific proof tied to `escrowCommitment`.

#### Relay Gas Model

This ERC defines two relay gas models. An implementation MAY support either or both.

##### Model A: Direct Relay Settlement

The relay calls `relayClaim` as `msg.sender` and pays gas out of pocket.
The contract deducts a fee (up to `maxRelayFee`) from the locked amount and transfers it to the relay as compensation.

Requirements:

- The relay MUST be compensated atomically within the same `relayClaim` transaction.
- The contract MUST NOT transfer more than `maxRelayFee` to the relay.
- If the actual gas cost exceeds `maxRelayFee`, the relay MAY choose not to submit the transaction. The protocol MUST NOT force the relay to operate at a loss.

##### Model B: Meta-Transaction Forwarding

The relay forwards a pre-signed meta-transaction on behalf of the claimant.
Gas is paid by the claimant's smart account, a paymaster (e.g., [ERC-4337](./eip-4337.md)), or another gas sponsorship mechanism external to this ERC.

Requirements:

- `maxRelayFee` SHOULD be set to `0` since the relay is not compensated from the locked amount.
- The `authorization` parameter in `relayClaim` MUST carry the meta-transaction signature or UserOperation proof that the settlement contract or account abstraction infrastructure can verify.
- The relay MUST NOT modify the signed calldata.

##### Model Selection

The two models are not mutually exclusive. A single `relayClaim` implementation can distinguish between them by inspecting `maxRelayFee`:

- `maxRelayFee > 0` implies Model A: the contract MUST compensate the relay.
- `maxRelayFee == 0` implies Model B: the contract MUST NOT attempt relay compensation and SHOULD assume gas is handled externally.

### Recommended Commitment Composition

This ERC intentionally leaves commitment internals flexible, but interoperable implementations SHOULD use stable hash composition rules.

#### `applicationCommitment`

For execution-oriented systems, a RECOMMENDED derivation is:

```solidity
applicationCommitment = keccak256(abi.encode(
    applicationId,
    parametersHash,
    constraintsHash,
    executionDeadline,
    salt
));
```

#### `escrowCommitment`

A RECOMMENDED derivation is:

```solidity
escrowCommitment = keccak256(abi.encode(
    claimSecretHash,
    outputDescriptorHash,
    salt
));
```

These are recommendations only and do not constrain implementations that use alternative commitment schemes.

### Core On-Chain Interface

Implementations MAY use any internal storage model, but a conforming contract MUST expose the following minimal conditional settlement extension interface.

```solidity
interface IAgentConditionalSettlementExtension {
    enum LockStatus {
        None,
        Locked,
        Settled,
        Refunded
    }

    event ConditionalLockSettled(
        bytes32 indexed channelId,
        bytes32 indexed lockId,
        bytes32 indexed proofType,
        bytes32 proofDigest
    );

    event ConditionalLockRefunded(
        bytes32 indexed channelId,
        bytes32 indexed lockId
    );

    function settleConditional(
        bytes32 channelId,
        ConditionalLock calldata lock,
        SettlementProofRef calldata proofRef,
        bytes calldata proof
    ) external;

    function refundConditional(
        bytes32 channelId,
        bytes32 lockId
    ) external;

    function lockStatus(
        bytes32 channelId,
        bytes32 lockId
    ) external view returns (LockStatus);

    function supportsConditionType(
        bytes32 conditionType
    ) external view returns (bool);

    function supportsProofType(
        bytes32 proofType
    ) external view returns (bool);
}
```

#### Required Semantics

A compliant implementation MUST satisfy the following:

- `settleConditional` MUST settle a lock only if:
  - the lock is valid,
  - `channelId`, `lock.channelId`, and `proofRef.channelId` are identical,
  - `proofRef.lockId` equals the derived `lockId` of `lock`,
  - the proof or attestation is valid under local implementation rules,
  - the lock has not already been settled or refunded, and
  - **the lock is included in, or deterministically derivable from, the latest valid host-framework state recognized by the authoritative adjudicator for `channelId`**. An implementation MUST NOT settle a lock that belongs to a superseded or revoked state.
- `refundConditional` MUST refund a lock when its expiry has passed and valid settlement has not completed.
- `lockStatus` MUST expose whether a lock is unsettled, settled, or refunded **from the perspective of this on-chain extension**. `LockStatus.None` means no on-chain materialization is known to this extension, and MUST NOT be interpreted as proof that the lock never existed off chain.

This ERC does not require `ConditionalLock` objects to be individually registered on chain during the happy path.
Implementations MAY settle them only when invoked during the host framework's lifecycle resolution path.

#### Interface Detection

Contracts implementing the core interface or any extension defined in this ERC SHOULD implement [ERC-165](./eip-165.md) and return `true` for the relevant interface identifiers.

### Optional Extension B: Private Claim Relay

An implementation that supports private claim relays SHOULD expose:

```solidity
interface IAgentPrivateClaimRelay {
    event ClaimRelayed(
        bytes32 indexed lockId,
        address indexed relayer,
        bytes32 indexed requestHash
    );

    function relayClaim(
        ClaimRelayRequest calldata request,
        bytes calldata proof,
        bytes calldata authorization
    ) external returns (bytes32 claimId);
}
```

Requirements:

- `authorization` MUST prove that the relay request was authorized by the claimant.
- The relay implementation MUST respect `deadline`, `maxRelayFee`, and `nonce`.
- The relay extension MAY be implemented by the adjudicator, a vault, or a dedicated relay contract.

Implementations SHOULD document:

- whether relays learn claimant metadata,
- whether relay requests are encrypted,
- what replay protections are used,
- whether relays can censor or delay claims.

### Note On Local Rebalancing Coordination

Local rebalancing coordination (capacity certificate exchange between channels sharing the same hub) is a useful throughput optimization for agent-heavy deployments, but it introduces terms that are not yet abstracted to a framework-independent level.

This topic is deferred to a companion ERC or non-normative appendix. Implementations that need local rebalancing SHOULD define their own certificate format and MUST ensure that rebalancing state does not override the canonical settlement state used by the host framework lifecycle.

## Rationale

### Design Goals

This ERC is designed to allow:

- Near-zero-gas happy-path settlement through off-chain signatures.
- Conditional settlement triggered by proofs, attestations, or timeouts.
- Strong replay protection across channels, chains, and intents.
- Compatibility with both EOAs and contract accounts.
- Composability with existing channel frameworks.
- Composability with [ERC-4337](./eip-4337.md) wallets, private relays, and application-specific backends.

The sections below explain the choices that follow from these goals.

### Why standardize the settlement layer instead of the full channel stack?

The full channel stack is still an active design space.
Different systems may choose HTLCs, virtual channels, programmable conditional locks, pairwise rebalancing, local rebalancing coordination, privacy-preserving hubs, or different dispute proofs.

Trying to standardize all of that in a single ERC would be too broad and would likely freeze experimentation too early.

By contrast, standardizing the settlement-facing envelope is immediately useful:

- wallets can produce a common lock format,
- hubs can advertise common capabilities,
- relays can serve many backends,
- adjudicators or companion extensions can expose a common integration surface.

### Why leave lifecycle and custody to the host framework?

Existing channel frameworks already define lifecycle and custody primitives.
Redefining those same primitives in this ERC would create unnecessary overlap, confuse implementers about which surface is authoritative, and make composability worse rather than better.

This ERC therefore standardizes the conditional settlement extension surface while explicitly delegating lifecycle and custody to the host framework.

### Why not just use an application protocol such as ERC-8183?

Application protocols such as [ERC-8183](./eip-8183.md) may standardize business objects, provider and evaluator roles, funding flows, submission flows, and terminal job outcomes.
This ERC does not attempt to replace those protocols, and it does not standardize a job, order, marketplace, subscription, or evaluator-driven commerce state machine.

Instead, it defines the lower-layer settlement envelope used when a proof-bound conditional obligation must be represented, proven, settled, refunded, or relayed inside or alongside an off-chain channel framework.

An [ERC-8183](./eip-8183.md)-like application can compose with this ERC, but conformance to this ERC does not require any job abstraction, evaluator role, or application-specific object model.

### Why EIP-712?

[EIP-712](./eip-712.md) provides explicit domain separation, human- and machine-readable structured messages, compatibility with EOAs and smart accounts, and a familiar signing model for wallets and agents.

### Why reuse ERC-5267 for domain discovery?

A custom `domainSeparator()` function duplicates functionality that [ERC-5267](./eip-5267.md) already standardizes. [ERC-5267](./eip-5267.md) is supported by major wallet SDKs and verification tooling, so reusing it reduces integration cost and avoids a parallel discovery mechanism.

Similarly, signatures from deployed contract signers are verified through [ERC-1271](./eip-1271.md), and [ERC-6492](./eip-6492.md) (counterfactual signature verification) covers signers whose contract is not yet deployed.

### Why define canonical condition type and proof type identifiers?

Without a shared set of type identifiers, different implementations would invent incompatible labels for the same settlement semantics. A hub receiving a `ConditionalLock` would be unable to determine whether it can evaluate the condition without implementation-specific out-of-band negotiation.

The two identifier families are intentionally orthogonal:

- `conditionType` answers **"what proposition must be true for settlement?"** — e.g., a preimage is revealed, a threshold of signers approved, an external entity asserted a fact.
- `proofType` answers **"how is that proposition proven?"** — e.g., via a ZK proof, an oracle signature, a TEE remote attestation.

This separation ensures that the same condition (e.g., `EXTERNAL_ASSERTION`) can be proven by different verification pathways (ZK, oracle, TEE) without requiring a combinatorial explosion of condition types. It also means new proof systems can be introduced without modifying the condition vocabulary.

Canonical identifiers use an open registry convention — `keccak256` over a human-readable label — rather than a fixed enumeration. This means:

- the initial set covers the most common condition semantics and verification pathways today,
- new types can be added by any party by following the `keccak256("NAMESPACE.TYPE")` convention,
- no governance process or ERC amendment is required to introduce a new type,
- implementations can use `supportsConditionType`, `supportsProofType`, and capability discovery to negotiate support dynamically.

### Why keep proof systems opaque?

The proof system used for conditional settlement is expected to evolve quickly.
Some implementations may use Merkle inclusion proofs, zk-SNARKs, oracle attestations, threshold signatures, or trusted execution attestations.

The ERC therefore standardizes the reference envelope, not the proof internals.

### Why is local rebalancing coordination deferred?

Local rebalancing coordination is useful for agent-heavy hubs because it reduces coordination fan-out.
However, it introduces implementation-specific terms that are not yet abstracted to a framework-independent level.
Deferring it to a companion ERC keeps the core standard focused on the settlement interface while allowing rebalancing designs to mature independently.

## Backwards Compatibility

This ERC is designed to be compatible with:

- [ERC-4337](./eip-4337.md) smart accounts and session-key systems,
- [ERC-1271](./eip-1271.md) contract-based signers,
- existing state channel frameworks that already provide lifecycle and custody primitives,
- existing payment channel implementations that already support close/dispute or challenge/conclude style resolution,
- private execution backends such as shielded pools or relay-based execution systems.

It does not require breaking changes to those systems.
It adds a common conditional settlement extension interface above them.

## Security Considerations

### Stale State And Replay

Locks, proof references, and relay requests are signed off chain and can be presented long after they were produced.
Without the replay protections required by the specification, a message could be presented again in another channel, on another chain, through another relay, after its expiry, or against a channel state the parties have already replaced, and the same funds could be paid out twice or paid out of a superseded state.

The typed data carries the fields needed to prevent this.
`channelId` binds a message to one settlement relationship, the [EIP-712](./eip-712.md) domain binds it to one chain and one verifying contract, `channelNonce` orders a lock within the channel so that a stale state can be recognized, and the relay request `nonce` and `deadline` limit how often and for how long a relay authorization can be used.

### Canonical Settlement State

Hubs commonly keep operational state, such as routing budgets or capacity budgets, next to the state the channel participants have signed.
If that operational state could influence settlement, a hub could settle against a view its counterparty never agreed to.
The specification therefore makes the latest valid state recognized by the host-framework adjudicator the only source of truth for settlement.

### Proof Verifier Trust

This ERC does not prove that a referenced proof verifier is trustworthy.
`settleConditional` is only as sound as the verifier contract named in `SettlementProofRef`: a faulty or malicious verifier can release any lock it is allowed to settle, and an upgradeable verifier can change its behavior after a lock has been signed.
This is why the specification requires implementations to define who chooses the verifier, how it is upgraded, and how the settlement root or proof anchor is authenticated.
Participants need that information before they co-sign a lock.

### Timeout Griefing

Conditional locks can be abused to consume liquidity or pending-lock slots.
A participant can open locks it never intends to settle, which ties up the counterparty's funds until each lock expires and costs the abuser little.
The limits recommended in the specification, on the number of pending locks, on lock value, and on expiry windows, bound the liquidity a single counterparty can immobilize and for how long.

### Private Claim Relay

Relays improve privacy but introduce additional trust and operational assumptions.
A relay sees the requests it is given unless they are encrypted, and it can censor or delay a claim.
A claim that is delayed past the `expiry` of its lock becomes refundable, so a claimant needs to leave enough time to settle directly or through another relay.
`maxRelayFee`, `deadline`, and `nonce` limit what a relay can take and how long it can hold a request, but they do not make a relay deliver.

### Adapter And Execution Safety

If a system uses adapters or execution routers behind this settlement interface, those adapters remain out of scope for the ERC but are security-critical in practice.
A permissive adapter can redirect or overspend settled funds, which is why the specification recommends strict allowlists, typed parameter validation, exact approvals, allowance reset, reentrancy protection, and output constraint checks for them.

### Privacy Considerations

This ERC can be used in both private and non-private systems.
By itself it does **not** guarantee hub blindness, amount privacy, unlinkability, claim anonymity, or side-channel resistance.

Those properties depend on optional routing, cryptographic, and relay layers built on top of this interface.
